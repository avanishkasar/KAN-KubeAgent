\subsection*{Offline results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} lists every policy under both protocols, and Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} places the learned gates and the patience rule on the trade-off between compute saved and regret. Five observations follow.

\paragraph{The gate transfers to unseen workloads} Trained on three workloads and applied to the fourth, the KAN gate saved \R{lowo}{kan}{saved}\% of all epochs (95\% CI \R{lowo}{kan}{savedlo} to \R{lowo}{kan}{savedhi}) at a mean regret of \R{lowo}{kan}{regret}\% (CI \R{lowo}{kan}{regretlo} to \R{lowo}{kan}{regrethi}), with \R{lowo}{kan}{prem}\% of runs stopped prematurely and a mean accuracy loss of \R{lowo}{kan}{acc} percentage points. Within a workload it saved \R{within}{kan}{saved}\% at \R{within}{kan}{regret}\% regret with \R{within}{kan}{prem}\% premature stops, so moving to an unseen workload cost no quality and about two percentage points of savings. Transfer works because every feature is normalised per run: a gate learns what a plateau looks like, not the loss level of a particular dataset. Of all learned gates, the KAN had the lowest mean regret and premature-stop rate under transfer (Table~\ref{tab:main}); the MLP and the depth-3 tree saved more compute but stopped \R{lowo}{mlp}{prem}\% and \R{lowo}{tree}{prem}\% of runs prematurely, and their regret was significantly higher ($p\T{lowo}{mlp}{regret}{p}$ and $p\T{lowo}{tree}{regret}{p}$, Table~\ref{tab:tests}).

\paragraph{Hindsight labels are what make the gate useful} The same KAN, trained on the same real decision points but with the labels of the hand-written rule, saved only \R{lowo}{kanrule}{saved}\% under transfer (\R{within}{kanrule}{saved}\% within workloads) and stopped runs significantly later ($p\T{lowo}{kanrule}{stop}{p}$), with no significant difference in regret ($p\T{lowo}{kanrule}{regret}{p}$). The rule itself, applied directly, stopped \R{lowo}{rule}{prem}\% of runs prematurely. Imitating a rule therefore produces a cautious copy of the rule's thresholds, while hindsight labels produce a gate that stops when the remaining improvement is actually small.

\paragraph{A well-chosen patience rule is as good on the stopping axis} Patience 8 saved \R{lowo}{pateight}{saved}\% at \R{lowo}{pateight}{regret}\% regret, and the regret of the KAN gate is not significantly different from it ($p\T{lowo}{pateight}{regret}{p}$); patience stopped slightly earlier ($p\T{lowo}{pateight}{stop}{p}$). On the frontier of Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} the patience family and the linear gate lie at or slightly above the KAN gate's curve, i.e.\ for the stop decision alone they reach the same regret with a few percentage points more compute saved. Shorter patience is clearly worse: patience 3 stopped \R{lowo}{patthree}{prem}\% of runs prematurely. The median stopping rule had a mean regret of \R{lowo}{median}{regret}\%, because in a grid of learning rates it stops slow-starting configurations that later become the best; it is designed for comparing trials, not for deciding when a single job has finished. The value of the KAN gate is therefore not a better stopping trade-off than a tuned patience rule. It is that a single learned rule, with no per-workload tuning, makes calibrated stop decisions on unseen workloads, with a tolerance expressed in units of regret, and is an explicit formula whose every decision can be recomputed and attributed.

\paragraph{A tuned patience rule under the same transfer protocol} Patience 8 was picked with knowledge of all four workloads. To compare like with like, we tuned the patience the way the gate is trained: for each held-out workload, the patience (1 to 20 epochs) that saves the most compute on the other three workloads while keeping their mean regret within a target was applied to the held-out one (Table~\ref{tab:pattransfer}). For targets of \X{PT@1@target}\% and \X{PT@2@target}\% the tuned rule saved \X{PT@1@saved}\% and \X{PT@2@saved}\% at \X{PT@1@regret}\% and \X{PT@2@regret}\% mean regret, so on the stop decision alone it is as good as or slightly better than the KAN gate. Its quality, however, transferred less evenly: the patience chosen on the three Adam-trained workloads was too short for the SGD-trained one, whose mean regret reached \X{PT@1@worst}\%, whereas the KAN gate's mean regret stayed at or below \X{PT@kanworst}\% on every held-out workload.

\paragraph{Every workload behaves consistently} Table~\ref{tab:perworkload} breaks the transfer result down by held-out workload. The KAN gate kept mean regret at or below 0.6\% on every held-out workload, including CIFAR-10, the only colour-image workload, and the SGD-trained MLP, the only workload with a different optimiser. Its largest single-run regret, \R{lowo}{kan}{max}\%, occurs on one unstable SGD run (learning rate 0.3) whose validation loss hovers without progress for about 15 epochs and then reaches its best value in a single isolated dip at epoch 21; every hindsight-trained gate and both patience 5 and patience 8 stop that run before the dip, and only the oracle does not.

The tolerance $\tau$ is a direct and predictable control of the trade-off: $\tau=2\%$ gives \Sw{2}{saved}\% saved at \Sw{2}{regret}\% regret and $\tau=10\%$ gives \Sw{10}{saved}\% at \Sw{10}{regret}\%, so an operator sets the acceptable loss of quality in the units in which it is measured.

\subsection*{Further checks}
Table~\ref{tab:checks} reports four further checks under the leave-one-workload-out protocol, with the same three gate seeds; the first row reproduces the main result exactly.

\paragraph{Decision cadence} In the main study the gate decides every two epochs and patience is checked after every epoch. Letting the gate decide after every epoch changed its result little (\X{RC@kan_every_epoch@saved}\% saved at \X{RC@kan_every_epoch@regret}\% regret; regret against the main gate $p\X{RC@p@kaneveryepoch@regret}$), and checking patience 8 only at the gate's decision epochs gave \X{RC@patience8_every2@saved}\% at \X{RC@patience8_every2@regret}\%. The difference in cadence therefore does not explain the comparison with patience.

\paragraph{Learning-curve extrapolation} A single-run extrapolation baseline fits a power law $v(t)=a+b\,t^{-c}$ to the validation losses seen so far at each decision epoch and stops when the fitted curve predicts less than $\tau/4=1.25\%$ further improvement of the best loss within the budget, the quantity the gate's stop band targets. It saved \X{RC@extrapolation@saved}\% of epochs but at \X{RC@extrapolation@regret}\% mean regret, with \X{RC@extrapolation@prem}\% of runs stopped prematurely: on these curves, which often plateau and then improve again, a parametric extrapolation from a partial curve is too optimistic about convergence. Ensembles of curve models~\cite{domhan2015extrapolation} or learned curve priors~\cite{adriaensen2023lcpfn} would be stronger, but they are fitted to rank configurations and their predictions are not readable rules.

\paragraph{Decision-level cost of symbolic fixing} On the held-out decision points, the spline network before symbolic fixing and the symbolic gate fell in the same decision band at \X{RC@agree}\% of points. Fixing the edges made the gate more conservative: the spline network saved \X{RC@kan_spline@saved}\% of epochs but at \X{RC@kan_spline@regret}\% regret with \X{RC@kan_spline@prem}\% premature stops ($p\X{RC@p@kanspline@regret}$ for regret against the symbolic gate). On these workloads the readable formula therefore costs no stopping quality, although it changes about a quarter of the individual decisions.

\paragraph{Feature constants} With window $w=3$, $5$ and $8$ the gate saved \X{RC@w3_n30@saved}\%, \X{RC@w5_n30@saved}\% and \X{RC@w8_n30@saved}\% at \X{RC@w3_n30@regret}\%, \X{RC@w5_n30@regret}\% and \X{RC@w8_n30@regret}\% regret, and with the $x_5$ normaliser at 20, 30 and 40 epochs it saved \X{RC@w5_n20@saved}\%, \X{RC@w5_n30@saved}\% and \X{RC@w5_n40@saved}\% at \X{RC@w5_n20@regret}\%, \X{RC@w5_n30@regret}\% and \X{RC@w5_n40@regret}\% regret. The constants move the gate along the same trade-off, as the tolerance $\tau$ does, without a loss of quality, so the default values are not a tuned optimum on which the results depend.
