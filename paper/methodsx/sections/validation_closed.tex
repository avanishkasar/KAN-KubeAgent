\subsection*{Closed-loop control of live training}
Offline replay cannot show what a learning-rate change does. We therefore let the complete controller (feature extraction, supervisor, gate, executor) control live training processes. For each workload we re-ran the 12 configurations of seed 2 under control, with the gate trained on the \emph{other three} workloads, and compared each controlled run with its uncontrolled recording, which has the same seed and settings. Control is asynchronous, as in deployment: the process keeps training while the controller decides, and a learning-rate change takes effect at the next epoch boundary. Until the first intervention, every controlled run reproduced its uncontrolled curve, which confirms that the comparison is paired.

\paragraph{Unseen workloads} Over all 48 runs the controller used \C{lowoall}{used} of \C{lowoall}{budget} epochs (\C{lowoall}{saved}\% saved) and stopped \C{lowoall}{stopped} runs early (Table~\ref{tab:closed}, Fig.~\ref{fig:closed}). The best validation loss was unchanged in the median (\C{lowoall}{relmed}\%) and not significantly different from uncontrolled training ($p\C{lowoall}{p}$); \C{lowoall}{better} runs ended more than 2\% better and \C{lowoall}{worse} more than 2\% worse, and the mean change in validation accuracy at the best checkpoint was $\C{lowoall}{acc}$ percentage points. Savings were lower than in offline replay of the same 48 configurations (\X{offlineSeedTwoSaved}\%) because a run's curve changes after a learning-rate halving, so the controlled runs are no longer the recorded ones, and because a stop decided asynchronously takes effect only at the process's next epoch boundary. The largest loss, \C{lowomlpsgd}{relmax}\%, is the unstable SGD run discussed above, which the gate stopped before its isolated late minimum.

\paragraph{The learning-rate guard} Without the guard the executor halved the learning rate up to \C{fmnistnoguard}{adjmax} times in one Fashion-MNIST run and up to \C{cifarnoguard}{adjmax} times in one CIFAR-10 run, leaving those runs at a small fraction of their initial learning rate. With the guard, at most \C{fmnistguard}{adjmax} halvings per run were executed (\C{fmnistguard}{deferred} further requests were deferred on Fashion-MNIST), and the worst single-run change in best validation loss fell from $+\C{fmnistnoguard}{relmax}\%$ to $+\C{fmnistguard}{relmax}\%$ on Fashion-MNIST and from $+\C{cifarnoguard}{relmax}\%$ to $+\C{lowocifar}{relmax}\%$ on CIFAR-10, at the cost of fewer epochs saved on Fashion-MNIST (\C{fmnistguard}{saved}\% against \C{fmnistnoguard}{saved}\%). Neither condition changed best validation loss significantly. The guard is a deployment safeguard rather than a learned component, and its two parameters (spacing and maximum count) should be revisited for jobs with very different schedules.

\subsection*{Comparison with standard practice in live training}
The usual way to automate both decisions in PyTorch or Keras is a reduce-on-plateau learning-rate schedule combined with patience-based early stopping. We ran this policy live on the same 48 configurations: the learning rate is halved (the same step as the controller) when validation loss has not improved by more than a relative $10^{-4}$ for 3 epochs, and the run stops after 8 epochs without improvement, the best patience of the offline study. Because that patience was chosen with knowledge of all four workloads, the comparison favours the baseline. We also ran a combination that the closed-loop results suggest: the KAN gate trained on the other three workloads decides only when to stop, at the usual decision epochs, and the learning rate follows the same reduce-on-plateau schedule. This variant was specified after the controller's and the baseline's live results were known, so its result is confirmatory only for these 48 configurations. Table~\ref{tab:plateau} reports all three policies, each paired run by run with the uncontrolled recording.

\paragraph{Standard practice is at least as good as the full controller} The reduce-on-plateau baseline used \X{PL@plat@used} of 1{,}920 epochs (\X{PL@plat@saved}\% saved), fewer than the KAN controller's \X{PL@kan@used} (paired Wilcoxon $p\X{PL@kanplat@pep}$), and it improved best validation loss over uncontrolled training ($p\X{PL@plat@p}$; \X{PL@plat@better} runs more than 2\% better, \X{PL@plat@worse} more than 2\% worse). The difference in best validation loss between the two controllers was not significant ($p\X{PL@kanplat@ploss}$). The controller's weaker point is its learning-rate action: the adjust band responds to runs that are still improving slowly, and on MNIST it halved the learning rate of runs whose learning rate was already too low, which slowed them further (up to \X{PL@kan@mnist@relmax}\% higher best validation loss on MNIST, against at most \X{PL@plat@mnist@relmax}\% for the baseline). A plateau schedule halves the learning rate only after the loss has stopped improving.

\paragraph{KAN stop with a plateau schedule} With the learning rate left to the plateau schedule and only the stop decision taken by the KAN gate, the controller used \X{PL@ks@used} epochs (\X{PL@ks@saved}\% saved), with a median change in best validation loss of \X{PL@ks@relmed}\% (\X{PL@ks@better} runs better, \X{PL@ks@worse} worse; $p\X{PL@ks@p}$ against uncontrolled). Against standard practice the paired differences were $p\X{PL@ksplat@pep}$ for epochs used and $p\X{PL@ksplat@ploss}$ for best validation loss. We therefore recommend deploying the gate as the stop rule, where its decisions are learned from outcomes and can be audited, and leaving the learning rate to a plateau schedule unless the adjust band has been validated in closed loop on the target kind of job.
